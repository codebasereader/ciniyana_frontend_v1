The Organic Relationship Between Kannada Theatre and Cinema

The relationship between Kannada theatre and cinema has a history spanning a century. Kannada cinema has grown by drawing upon the strength and richness of theatre. On the occasion of World Theatre Day (March 27), here is an extensive article by National Award-winning writer Dr. K. Puttaswamy.

K. Puttaswamy, Writer

Kannada theatre, like Indian theatre, emerged from the folk traditions of the people. Ancient theatre is generally classified into two categories. One was folk theatre created by communities for their own entertainment. In Karnataka, forms such as Bayalata, Sannata, Doddata, and Gombeyata belong to this tradition. The other was theatre patronised by royalty. Since supporting the arts was considered one of the duties of kings, theatrical performances were regularly organised in royal courts. These performances imitated Sanskrit theatre and therefore gave greater importance to music and acting. Interestingly, most of the artistes were also musicians. Neither of these two forms of theatre was ever an industry. They did not follow any established economic model. Their primary objectives were entertainment and education.

From the latter half of the nineteenth century, professional theatre companies began emerging in India. They deserve credit for transforming theatre into an industry through public entertainment. Theatre thus acquired an institutional structure. In particular, Parsi theatre not only pioneered professional theatre in India but also played a significant role in shaping the growth and development of Indian professional theatre and, later, cinema. Even today, cinema continues to grapple with the influence of Parsi theatre.

The Parsi community gained economic prosperity through commerce and shipbuilding. Primarily settled along the western coast, the community expanded its activities into various fields. Philanthropy and culture were two major areas in which they made significant contributions. As merchants, they naturally began transforming theatre into an industry. They built theatres for public performances, recruited artistes on monthly salaries, encouraged musicians to experiment, and commissioned writers to create and stage plays.

The role played by the Parsi community in shaping Indian theatre was unparalleled. Theatres where plays were performed existed in areas of Mumbai heavily populated by Parsis. These venues were known as “Peela Houses,” a corruption of the term “Play House.” As Parsi theatre companies travelled to distant towns, they ignited a passion for theatre wherever they went. Each flame they lit eventually began speaking the language of its respective region.

Parsi theatre remained at its peak from 1850 to 1930. Borrowing material, themes, and design elements from various theatrical traditions around the world, it created a distinctive form of theatre. Primarily entertaining the middle class, professional theatre became a medium for generating economic profit. Unlike folk theatre, whose objectives included worship, moral instruction, and entertainment, and court theatre, which emphasised artistic sophistication and aesthetic appreciation, professional theatre focused on entertainment and commercial success. Its theatrical methods reflected these priorities.

Parsi theatre adapted British stage design and visual composition, European theatrical staging, Indian mythological themes, Western plays, and stories from Arab countries to the Indian context.

Along with stories from the Ramayana, Mahabharata, and other mythological sources, Parsi theatre organisers adapted subjects such as Shakespeare’s Romeo and Juliet and Arabic tales like Gul Bakavali, Sohrab Rustum, and Aladdin for the stage. For the costumes, makeup, and visual representation of mythological characters, they drew inspiration from the canvases of artist Raja Ravi Varma, who had painted mythological figures. They brought every detail—from costumes, jewellery, and crowns to the finest visual elements of those paintings—to life on stage.

They used the same visual foundation for designing the costumes of mythological and historical figures. The way historical characters such as Basavanna of the twelfth century, Sri Krishnadevaraya of the fourteenth century, and Chhatrapati Shivaji are remembered in popular imagination wearing crowns, armlets, and elaborate costumes similar to those of Raja Ravi Varma’s gods owes much to the influence of Parsi theatre.

It was largely under the influence of Parsi theatre that professional theatre companies emerged in Karnataka as well. In addition, the first professional theatre company in Gadag was established as a response to the influence of Marathi theatre. Founded in 1874 under the leadership of Shantakavi Balacharya Gopalacharya Sakkari, the Sri Veeranarayana Prasadita Kritapura Nataka Mandali was the first professional Kannada theatre company. It later gave rise to the Royal Dramatic Company.

Although palace theatre companies performed plays for the public, independent theatre companies were established in several parts of southern Karnataka. Companies such as the Rajadhani Nataka Mandali founded by Rangacharyas in Mandya (1881), Rasika Manollasini Nataka Mandali (1883), Sri Saraswathi Vilasa Karnataka Sabha, Bengaluru’s Sri Kanteshwara Karnataka Nataka Sabha, Gollarapete Nataka Sabha, and Bullappa’s Company emerged in Mysuru, Bengaluru, and surrounding areas during the early period.

However, some individuals capitalised on the artistic appreciation that professional Kannada theatre had cultivated among the public and, through organisational skill and continuous experimentation, built large institutions. These included the Sri Gubbi Channabasaveshwara Kripaposhita Nataka Sangha, popularly known as the Gubbi Company; Sri Sahitya Samrajya Nataka Mandali, also known as the S.S.S. Company or Subbaiah Naidu Company; Tiger Varadacharya’s Ratnavali Theatricals; Peer Saheb’s Chandrakala Nataka Mandali; Hirannaiah Mitra Mandali; and several others.

From the 1880s, professional Kannada theatre not only established deep roots but also spread across South India. Growing freely beyond linguistic boundaries, Kannada theatre gave the nation outstanding playwrights, artistes, and business managers. While professional theatre productions became popularly known as “Company Natakas,” professional theatre itself became permanently associated in the public mind with the term “Nataka Company.” These institutions and this medium introduced audiences to numerous possibilities in entertainment.

Just as professional theatre influenced Indian cinema, it had a profound influence on Kannada cinema as well. The Indian cinematic tradition began with Dhundiraj Govind Phalke’s Raja Harishchandra (1913). Phalke, who came from a traditional Chitpavan Brahmin family and had trained in sculpture and painting rather than theatre, chose cinema as his field of experimentation. For his first film, he selected the familiar story of Harishchandra, which had already been successfully presented through Harikatha and theatre.

However, during the silent-film era, most films that followed placed less emphasis on narrative. Cinema, a new technological medium, was created primarily to attract audiences. Filmmakers incorporated scenes designed to shock, amaze, and amuse viewers. They combined adventure and dance sequences with simple storylines.

After the arrival of the talkies, all this changed dramatically in India. Films began to be made as extended forms of theatre. They incorporated the clearly defined characters, intense emotional elements, visually captivating scenes, and moral lessons of traditional Indian theatre based on the nine rasas.

In its early years, Indian cinema did not give complete importance to narrative. Later, the M.V.S.–R.N.R. duo, with the support of the A.V.M. organisation, created history with Satya Harishchandra. Here too, the performances of Subbaiah Naidu as Harishchandra and Raya as Vishwamitra captivated audiences.

The last film on which the two worked together was Mahatma Kabir (1947). Directed by Raya, the film featured Naidu as Kabir. After the M.V.S.–R.N.R. partnership ended, M.S. Subbaiah Naidu, under the banner of Sri Sahitya Samrajya Nataka Mandali, directed Bhakta Prahlada (1958) along with H.S. Krishnaswamy. It was his final film as an actor. His son Lokesh played Prahlada in the film. It was also the film that marked director V. Somashekar’s entry into cinema. Lokesh, who later became a celebrated figure in amateur theatre, went on to become one of the rare and distinguished actors of Kannada cinema.

Indian cinema initially relied heavily on mythological stories, historical events, and the enchanting world of folklore. Indian audiences were accustomed to repeatedly returning to their traditional mythological stories, folk worlds, and historical settings for pleasure. Rather than saying that filmmakers simply exploited the audience’s fascination with mythology and history, it would be more appropriate to say that they avoided risks and adapted familiar, established theatrical narratives to the screen.

Theatre had succeeded in attracting audiences by exaggerating mythological and historical stories and creating an illusionary world. Cinema, an extraordinary medium capable of creating three-dimensional visual experiences on a two-dimensional screen, expanded the creative possibilities of theatre even further.

As a result, cinema succeeded in creating a much larger world of illusion than theatre. Close-up shots, multiple camera angles, outdoor locations, retakes, editing, and post-production—all unavailable to theatre—enhanced cinema’s capabilities. Although plays and films may be based on the same story, their effects are different and they create entirely different worlds of experience. Hence, early films were largely based on familiar stories from mythology, history, and folklore.

India’s first film, Alam Ara (1931), was made in Hindi and was based on a Parsi play of the same name that had already been successful on stage. The first Tamil film, Kalidas (1931), was also based on a play. The first Telugu film, Bhakta Prahlada (1932), too originated as a theatrical work. The first Bengali film, Dena Paona (1931), was the exception, as it was based on a novel by Sarat Chandra Chattopadhyay.

The first Kannada talkie experiment also began with a theatrical work. Although Sati Sulochana is credited as the first Kannada film to be released, Bhakta Dhruva holds the distinction of being the first Kannada-language film to go on floors. Bhakta Dhruva was the second film to be released. In any case, most of the artistes and technicians involved in both films had backgrounds in theatre.

Before this, it is important to mention the relationship between professional Kannada theatre and other modern playwrights with cinema. Some film historians believe that the silent-film era in Karnataka began in 1921. It is estimated that around 175 silent films may have been made between 1921 and 1933.

It is said that the then young Maharaja of Mysuru, Sri Kantheerava Narasimharaja Wadiyar, filmed Nirupama (1921), a play performed by Ratnavali Theatrical Company, in which A.V. Varadacharya acted. The play was filmed using a hand-cranked camera. Special screenings of this “play film” were reportedly organised for Indian and foreign dignitaries visiting the palace. Thus, A.V. Varadacharya may be considered the first leading actor of a silent film shot on Kannada soil, while Kantheerava Narasimharaja Wadiyar could be regarded as its first producer.

The silent film Vasanta Sena (1925) was a milestone in the history of cinema in Karnataka. Its production began under unusual circumstances. The people who initiated the project were Mohan Bhavanani and playwright T.P. Kailasam.

Mohan Bhavanani was a cameraman trained in Germany and a North Indian associated with the Central Film Division. During a conversation with T.P. Kailasam, he reportedly proposed making a feature film in Mysuru. Kailasam suggested adapting Shudraka’s play Mrichchhakatika. Bhavanani agreed with the idea of adapting this timeless masterpiece of Indian literature. T.P. Kailasam played Shakara, one of the principal characters in the film. The film was screened not only across India but also abroad.

Writers and playwrights such as Sriranga and Devudu Narasimha Shastry also took an interest in film production. Devudu reportedly inspired Gubbi Veeranna to produce several silent films. A silent film titled Kallara Koota was also made based on one of his stories.

The renowned M.V. Subbaiah Naidu, who was prominent during the 1930s and 1940s, was the lead actor of the first Kannada talkie, Sati Sulochana. Bellave Narahari Shastri was the first film writer. Actor R. Nagendra Rao, who composed and provided music for sixteen songs, was the first music director. Lakshmibai, who sang the song “Bhale Bhale Parvati Balu Chature,” was the first playback singer of Kannada cinema.

M.V. Subbaiah Naidu, who played the role of antagonist Indrajitu in Sati Sulochana, was a classical artiste of both Kannada theatre and cinema. He conducted many experiments in theatre and brought discipline and glamour to the stage. Because of his strong attachment to theatre, he could not become deeply involved in the still-young film industry. Nevertheless, during his time in cinema, he created a distinctive acting style. His innovative approach to portraying mythological characters is said to have influenced many actors who followed.

Subbaiah Naidu had acted in Gubbi Veeranna’s silent film His Love Affair. He later joined hands with another distinguished theatre artiste, R. Nagendra Rao, to establish the Sri Sahitya Samrajya Mandali. Naidu and Raya ran the organisation like brothers for seventeen years before eventually parting ways.

The Naidu–Raya partnership also produced significant experiments in cinema. With Raya’s connections to Tamil film producers and Naidu as his partner, they entered film production. In the 1941 film Vasantasena, Naidu played Charudatta, while Raya gave a new interpretation to the role of Shakara.

Kannada cinema introduced social themes on a large scale to an Indian film industry that had largely relied on mythology, history, and devotional stories. The 1931 film Samsara Nauka dealt with contemporary domestic life. It too was based on a theatrical work.

H.L.N. Simha, who brought a major turning point to Kannada cinema through Bedara Kannappa, was an outstanding talent of both Kannada theatre and cinema. Tired of long mythological plays that continued through the night, Simha decided to write a social drama. After joining Peer Saheb’s Chandrakala Nataka Mandali, he wrote the three-and-a-half-hour play Samsara Nauka (1931). It was a play that helped theatre move away from the heavy dependence on music towards prose dramas. The success of Samsara Nauka on stage was successfully repeated in its cinematic adaptation.

In the early days, theatre companies, theatre artistes, and organisers deserve much of the credit for preserving and developing Kannada cinema. Among the important theatre institutions and personalities were the Gubbi Company, Sri Sahitya Samrajya Mandali, B.R. Panthulu, and B.S. Ranga. The contribution of Mahatma Pictures, which emerged without a theatre background, is equally worthy of remembrance.

The Sri Gubbi Channabasaveshwara Kripaposhita Nataka Sangha, established in 1884, became the only professional theatre organisation in the world to celebrate its centenary. It became permanently known to the public as the Gubbi Nataka Company, or simply the Gubbi Company.

In 1896, six-year-old G.H. Veeranna left his education and joined the company as an artiste. Initially performing comedy roles, Veeranna took over ownership of the company at the age of 27. He conducted numerous experiments in Kannada theatre and achieved considerable success. Possessing exceptional business skills, he led the company efficiently.

Veeranna believed that theatre and cinema should grow in a mutually supportive manner. Even before the talkies began, he recognised the importance of cinema. The experiments he conducted in both fields remain evidence of his courage and creativity.

After establishing Gubbi Karnataka Films, Veeranna continued producing films such as Sadarame (1935), Subhadra (1941), Jeevana Nataka (1942), Hemareddy Mallamma (1945), and Gunasagari (1953).

The Gubbi Karnataka Films production Bedara Kannappa (1954) changed the course of Kannada cinema. The Gubbi organisation also operated as a distribution company. In addition, it owned the Geetha, Movieland, and Sagar theatres on Kempegowda Road in Bengaluru. Thus, the Gubbi Company contributed to cinema through production, distribution, and exhibition. Another important chapter in its history was the establishment of Sri Kanteerava Studio through Gubbi Veeranna.

The Gubbi Company was a great training ground. Many artistes and technicians who emerged from its ranks went on to leave their own mark on Kannada cinema. Director H.L.N. Simha, who directed the first Kannada social film and later transformed Kannada cinema through Bedara Kannappa, had links with the Gubbi Company.

Writers Ku. Ra. Seetharama Shastry and Bellave Narahari Shastry, producer-director B.R. Panthulu, who earned a major reputation in South India, R. Nagendra Rao, and M.V. Subbaiah Naidu, who later established another organisation, were all associated with the Gubbi Company.

The professional career of B.V. Karanth, who later conducted significant experiments in Kannada theatre and cinema, also began with the Gubbi Company. The company provided a platform for the cinematic interests of writers such as Devudu Narasimha Shastry.

The only film in which Rajkumar’s father, Singanalluru Puttaswamaiah, acted was also a Gubbi Company production. Before Bedara Kannappa, Veeranna provided opportunities in Kannada cinema to exceptional artistes such as Honnappa Bhagavathar and D. Kemparaj Urs. Bedara Kannappa in particular helped Kannada cinema gain tremendous momentum.

The film marked the major entry of Rajkumar, T.R. Narasimharaju, and G.V. Iyer into cinema. In addition, Udayakumar, Balakrishna, B.R. Raghavendra Rao, Eshwarappa, Mahabala Raya, C.B. Mallappa, Dikki Madhavarao, and Tamasha Madhavarao were all products of the Gubbi Company.

M.V. Vasudeva Rao, the first Kannada artiste to win a National Award for acting, grew up as a child artiste in the Gubbi Company. Master Nataraj, who won the State and National Best Child Actor awards for his role as Kitty in Kaadu, was Veeranna’s grandson.

The first talkie heroine Tripuramba, renowned singer K. Ashwathamma, Swarnamma, and several others also entertained audiences on stage.

Several members of the Gubbi Company family went on to contribute significantly to cinema. Basavaraj, G.V. Shivanand’s son Nataraj, Padmashree, B. Jayashree, and Latadevi worked in various areas of Kannada cinema.

Master Nataraj, who played Kitty in Kaadu, brought Kannada its first National Award for Best Child Actor. Even today, tracing the family tree of the Gubbi Company reveals branches spread across Kannada cinema and television.

Thus, the Gubbi Company was a great training ground for talented people, and its contribution ultimately enriched Kannada cinema. It became a centre for training artistes and technicians, while also contributing significantly to film production and distribution. It was a rare institution that nurtured both theatre and cinema equally.

One of the two owners of the Sri Sahitya Samrajya Mandali, R. Nagendra Rao, later established a model of naturalistic acting in Kannada cinema. He directed well-regarded films such as Namma Makkalu. He also placed his three sons in different areas of cinema.

R.N. Jayagopal, who trained under him, became one of our finest lyricists, dialogue writers, and directors. R.N. Krishnaprasad was the cinematographer of films such as Naandi, Bellimoda, and Naguvahuvu. In the song “Moodala Maneya Muthina…” from Bellimoda, he created what can be described as visual poetry. Though Krishnaprasad made a distinctive mark as a director through Naguvahuvu, he did not establish himself permanently in that field. R.N. Sudarshan enjoyed several years of success as a leading actor.

B. R. Panthulu, born in 1911 in Buduguru village in Kuppam, Andhra Pradesh, was an exceptional talent in Indian theatre and cinema. Driven by his passion for theatre, he left his teaching profession and joined Peer Saheb’s Chandrakala Nataka Samaja. He became famous by acting in popular plays such as Samsara Nauke, Sadarame, and Gulebakavali Kathe. His role as Sundara in Samsara Nauke made him popular.

He later joined the Gubbi Nataka Company. As the lead actor of Samsara Nauke, the first major Kannada social film, B.R. Panthulu, along with Raya and Subbaiah Naidu, helped establish an early model of acting. He did not possess Raya’s restraint or Subbaiah Naidu’s naturally theatrical intensity; instead, he maintained a balance between the two.

In fact, the acting styles created by these three actors remained influential and emulated for several decades.

In 1955, Panthulu established his own production company, Padmini Pictures. Between 1955 and 1972, he produced a total of twenty Kannada films and directed eighteen. Apart from two historical films, two mythological films, one folk film, and one children’s film, all his other films were social dramas.

School Master was one of his finest works. It was a response to the social conditions of post-Independence India. It addressed rural education, the position of the teacher, the younger generation’s rejection of old traditions as they adapted to urban life, the growing menace of dowry, and the changing relationship between urbanised children and their parents.

The film captured a broad canvas of social disintegration. Panthulu had a particular interest in exploring the emotional threads connecting family members. The man who gave audiences an extraordinary film like Sri Krishnadevaraya was a major talent who enriched cinema through his theatrical background.

When Kannada cinema’s activities came to a standstill in the 1960s, artistes who had moved from theatre to cinema became disheartened. Theatre came to the rescue of artistes who were struggling with unemployment and helplessness.

Kannada artistes formed a cooperative society and returned to their old field—theatre. Rajkumar, Narasimharaju, Balakrishna, and G.V. Iyer did not hesitate to act on stage again. Audiences also encouraged them.

However, they felt they should not sever their connection with cinema. They invested the money they earned from theatre and entered film production. Under the banner of Kannada Kalavidaru, Rajkumar, T.N. Narasimharaju, T.R. Balakrishna, and G.V. Iyer joined hands to begin production of Ranadheera Kanteerava.

Thus, Ranadheera Kanteerava was not only a unique historical film but also an important testament to the deep, organic relationship between theatre and cinema.

In 1925, the Gubbi Channabasaveshwara Company filmed the play Mahatma Kabir, which was being staged in Tarikere, and turned it into a film. The lead role of Kabir was played by Abhinava Shiromani C.B. Mallappa.

From the beginning of the twentieth century, many theatre companies had flourished across Karnataka. Their artistes filled the shortage of performers in the early days of cinema. At a time when women were hesitant to act in films, these theatre artistes in particular filled the shortage of heroines and female supporting actors.

During the golden age of professional theatre, acclaimed actresses such as Lakshmibai, Kamalabai, Malavalli Sundaramma, Ashwathamma, B. Jayamma, Gangubai Guleddagudda, Ameerbai Karnataki, who later became famous as a singer in Mumbai cinema, M.V. Rajamma, M. Fandari Bai, Ballari Lalitha, Harini, Ballari Ratnamala, B. Jayashree, and many other senior actresses were all trained in theatre.

Most of the early leading men not only came from theatre but also served Kannada cinema for many years. From M.S. Subbaiah Naidu, R.N.R., B.R. Panthulu, Kemparaj Urs, and Honnappa Bhagavathar to T.N. Balakrishna, G.V. Iyer, Narasimharaju, Belur Raghavendra Rao, H.R. Shastry, Kuppuraj, Siddaiah Swamy, Vasudeva Girimaji, Kaushik Dikki Madhavarao, and Hanumantharao, artistes migrated from theatre to cinema and brought diversity to the film industry.

The leading actors of the Kannada cinema renaissance—Dr. Rajkumar, Udayakumar, Kalyankumar (whose theatre experience was comparatively less than that of the first two), and K.S. Ashwath—also entered cinema with rich theatrical experience.

The Kumaratraya, K.S. Ashwath, Balakrishna, Narasimharaju, G.V. Iyer, and other theatre talents elevated Kannada cinema to a higher level.

The literary foundation of cinema, particularly dialogue and lyric writing, was also laid by people trained in theatre. Bellave Narahari Shastry, who wrote plays for the Gubbi Company, wrote the dialogues and songs for the first Kannada talkie, Sati Sulochana.

Another playwright, B. Puttaswamaiah, contributed literary material to films such as Subhadra and Jeevana Nataka. Among the prominent early film writers were veteran playwrights such as Hirannaiah, Ku. Ra. Si., M. Narendrababu, and R.N.R., who brought their theatrical experience into cinema.

Before becoming a film writer through Krishna Leela (1947), Hunsur Krishnamurthy had written plays such as Gopichand, Echamanayaka, and Dallali. He had worked with several theatre companies. Later, during the 1950s, he wrote for a large number of films and gradually entered film production and direction.

Thus, talents who were equally passionate about Kannada theatre and cinema played a major role in taking Kannada cinema forward.

However, during the 1960s, the migration of theatre talents into cinema largely came to a halt. There were several reasons. Professional theatre companies were forced to retreat under the influence of cinema. By then, the film industry had begun attracting people from various fields. Directors did not seriously consider making use of theatrical experience.

Although a few theatre talents occasionally entered cinema, the large-scale migration from theatre seen earlier became rare. Actors from amateur theatre, such as Srinath and Gangadhar, were among the exceptions.

At the same time, the emerging amateur theatre movement viewed cinema with a certain degree of prejudice. There was a prevailing perception that cinema was crude and lacked the subtlety of amateur theatre. That was why C.R. Simha, who acted in Sankalpa, produced and directed by P.V. Nanjraja Urs, once said that theatre was superior to cinema and rejected opportunities that came his way after the film’s release. (His later return to cinema is another story.)

The bond between theatre and cinema was renewed in the 1970s. This time, amateur theatre made a greater contribution to cinema.

Samskara was a landmark film created by talents from amateur theatre. Girish Karnad, P. Lankesh, Snehalatha Reddy, Dasharathi Dikshit, and actors and actresses from amateur theatre groups in Bengaluru and Madras, who were involved in Kannada and English theatre, acted in the film.

The success of Samskara opened the doors to a major migration of amateur theatre talent into cinema. Many of the parallel films that followed Samskara were produced, directed, and acted in by people from amateur theatre.

Girish Kasaravalli, who studied direction at the Film and Television Institute of India in Pune, and writer Baraguru Ramachandrappa were among the exceptions.

Several personalities who later created films recognised nationally and internationally—including Girish Karnad, B.V. Karanth, T.S. Ranga, M.B.N. Prasad, T.S. Nagabharana, Chandrashekhara Kambara, M.S. Sathyu, C.R. Simha, and actors such as Anant Nag, Venkatarao Talagere, L.V. Sharada, Shankar Nag, Lokesh, Vaishali Kasaravalli, Arundhati Nag, Umashree, Suresh Heblikar, T.N. Seetharam, H.G. Somashekara Rao, Dattatreya, C.H. Lokanath, Kokila Mohan, Devaraj, Sundarraj, Kashi, and Jagadish Malnad—came from amateur theatre.

In the technical fields too, people such as C. Ashwath (music director), Shashidhar Adapa (art), Ramesh Desai (art), Vaishali Kasaravalli (costume design), Jayashree (singer and actress), and T.N. Narasimhan (actor and director) brought greater creativity and originality to Kannada cinema.

Thus, Kannada cinema continued to grow by drawing strength from the richness and vitality of theatre.

In recent years, theatre activities have gained considerable momentum across Karnataka. A large number of enthusiastic young people have entered the field. Both cinema and television have grown into industries.

For many, the primary attractions are the instant fame and money these fields offer, rather than a love for art, a deep engagement with it, or the desire to explore something new. This may not apply to everyone, but it is true of a large section of those involved in these fields.

People with little understanding of visual composition are directing mega-serials, while acting is often reduced to mechanically delivering lines. There is a growing number of people eager to appear in television serials and reality shows that remain miles away from intellectual engagement.

Cinema, meanwhile, has largely surrendered to the menace of remakes. Real-estate money and underground money behind film production are driven by motivations very different from the pursuit of artistic exploration. As a result, there is little concern for quality.

In such circumstances, theatre companies are increasingly becoming training grounds that supply artistes and technicians to cinema and television. Even if they are few in number, it is fortunate for Kannadigas that amateur theatre groups with a genuine commitment to theatre continue to remain active.

Thus, the relationship between Kannada theatre and cinema continues unabated.
